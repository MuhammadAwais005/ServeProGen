A first version of a SaaS product should be small. Its foundations should not be fragile. A handful of architectural decisions are cheap on day one and painful to change after the tenth customer.

## Decide how tenants are isolated

Every SaaS product serves several customers from one system. The core question is how their data is kept apart.

| Approach | Isolation | Operational cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shared tables with a tenant column | Logical | Lowest |
| Schema per tenant | Stronger | Medium |
| Database per tenant | Strongest | Highest |

For most early products, shared tables with a tenant key are enough, provided every query is scoped automatically rather than by remembering to filter.

## Make tenant scoping impossible to forget

Put the tenant filter in one place, such as a model manager or a request-level context, so a new endpoint can't accidentally return another customer's records. Add tests that try to read across tenants and expect a failure.

## Roles belong to memberships

A person can belong to more than one organisation with different roles. Modelling roles on the membership, not the user, avoids an awkward migration later.

## Plan for subscriptions, even before billing

You may not charge on day one, but the product should know which plan a tenant is on and what that plan allows. Feature limits checked in one place make pricing changes a configuration task instead of a code hunt.

## Keep an audit trail

Recording who changed what, and when, helps support, security reviews and customer trust. It is far easier to start logging early than to reconstruct history later.

## Takeaways

Keep the first version focused on value, but invest a few days in isolation, roles, plans and auditing. They are the parts that are hardest to change once customers depend on them.
